Now I am at a loss to conceive, what influence or biass a judge of the supream court can be laid under to any suitor or other person or persons in Pennsylvania, by his being a delegate in congress, or a member of council or assembly for any other state: or how this can in the least affect his judicial conduct. In a political light, it can be no disadvantage to the interests or public weal of Pennsylvania to have ever so many of her citizens members of congress for other states. As to the vote of the house of assembly of the 24th of February, 1779, quoted by Tenax, I would premise, that, though every man is obliged to know the laws of the country he resides in, and to square his conduct by them, yet no man is legally bound to know or take notice of any resolve, vote, or minute of an house of assembly, unless duly served with a properly authenticated copy thereof; and then only in particular cases. As I have been well informed, no such copy was ever served upon or delivered to the chief justice, I flatter myself it will be ceded by all men, that in point of law he was not bound to know any thing of it; and I am authorized to declare, that, after this hasty vote passed, many of the forty-two, sensible of the error, applied to the chief justice personally, and advised him to continue in congress, informing him, that the whole of them, a very few excepted, had altered their opinion; and further, that none of the members ever expressed the least dissatisfaction with him on that account, but on the contrary were pleased with his conduct. Indeed, the vote was founded in surprize and error, and would not bear scrutiny or examination. A member of congress and a judge is clothed with considerable power and honour; and no man ought to be deprived of either, more than of property or liberty (even if they were no ways involved in this question) but by the law of the land, or the judgment of his peers. Natural justice requires, that no man should be condemned without being heard; and as no such opportu nity was afforded, the vote must be considered as the bare opinion of the house on a mere speculative question, and could not possibly be attended with any other operation.
