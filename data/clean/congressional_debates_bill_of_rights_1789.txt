CONGRESSIONAL DEBATES ON THE BILL OF RIGHTS
1789

LEGISLATIVE TIMELINE AND PROCESS

JUNE 8, 1789: MADISON'S INTRODUCTION
On June 8, 1789, James Madison introduced his proposed amendments to the House of Representatives. His initial proposals consisted of nine amendment articles, though these were subdivided into thirty-nine separate items when counted individually.

Key Points from Madison's Introduction:
- Addressed that a "respectable number of our constituents" desired amendments
- Stated that Congress should "incorporate such amendments in the Constitution as will secure those rights, which they consider as not sufficiently guarded"
- Argued that the amendments would satisfy opponents without altering the structure or powers of the general government
- Emphasized that these amendments aimed at protecting individual rights, not restructuring government powers

---

EARLY HOUSE DEBATES (JUNE-AUGUST 1789)

THE OPPOSITION EMERGES

Not all representatives were convinced of the necessity of amendments. Significant opposition came from:

ROGER SHERMAN OF CONNECTICUT
Sherman, a leading Federalist and signer of both the Declaration and the Constitution, objected to the amendments on multiple grounds:

1. Constitutional Authority Question: Sherman argued that Congress had no authority to propose changes to the wording of the Constitution itself
2. State Bills of Rights: Sherman reassured the Convention that the states had their own bills of rights and so had no need for a national bill of rights
3. Document Inviolability: Sherman persuaded the House to place the amendments at the Constitution's end rather than interpolating them within the text, so that the document would "remain inviolate"

THE DEBATE POSITIONS

Some legislators saw a bill of rights as:
- Unneeded (already protected by state constitutions and structure of federal government)
- Unworkable (any list of rights might be incomplete or restrictive)
- Unnecessary (certain rights could not be abridged by the federal government)

Others, led by Madison, argued:
- A bill of rights would educate the people about their fundamental freedoms
- It would provide an independent judiciary with clear authority to protect rights
- It would prevent more drastic constitutional changes by satisfied opponents
- It would publicly commit the government to fundamental principles

---

SPECIFIC AMENDMENTS DEBATED

FREEDOM OF CONSCIENCE AND SPEECH

The House debated whether to include protections for religious freedom and freedom of speech separately or together. The final language "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press" represented a compromise between various proposed formulations.

Original Madison proposal for religious freedom:
"The civil rights of none shall be abridged on account of religious belief or worship, nor shall any national religion be established..."

SECOND AMENDMENT - RIGHT TO BEAR ARMS

Madison's original formulation stated:
"The right of the people to keep and bear arms shall not be infringed; a well armed and well regulated militia being the best security of a free country: but no person religiously scrupulous of bearing arms shall be compelled to render military service in person."

The clause protecting conscientious objectors was removed during the legislative process.

The final language focused on the militia context without the religious exemption that Madison included, though the intent to protect both the militia and individual arms-bearing remained central to the debate.

DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS

The House consolidated Madison's various due process proposals into what became the Fifth Amendment, protecting against:
- Double jeopardy
- Self-incrimination
- Deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process
- Taking of private property without just compensation

GRAND JURY REQUIREMENT

The requirement for a grand jury presentment or indictment in capital crimes was debated, with some arguing it was unnecessary federal intrusion into state criminal procedures.

TRIAL BY JURY

Madison proposed that jury trials be guaranteed "in all criminal prosecutions by the laws of the land." The final language guaranteed this right but with exceptions for military cases.

---

COMMITTEE WORK AND COMPROMISES

HOUSE SELECT COMMITTEE

A committee was appointed to review Madison's proposals. The committee:

1. Eliminated most of Madison's preamble language about the source of government authority
2. Consolidated and refined the language of various amendments
3. Added the phrase "freedom of speech, and of the press" as separate elements
4. Removed the conscientious objector clause from the Second Amendment
5. Refined due process and trial language

ELEVEN-MEMBER COMMITTEE CHANGES

The committee made significant changes to Madison's nine proposed amendments, including:

1. Streamlining language for clarity
2. Removing redundancies between amendments
3. Adding specificity to protections
4. Narrowing scope in some cases (e.g., trial by jury limited to cases exceeding twenty dollars value)

---

HOUSE PASSAGE

AUGUST 24, 1789: HOUSE APPROVES 17 AMENDMENTS

The House of Representatives approved 17 amendments based on Madison's proposals, though they had been consolidated and refined. These 17 amendments represented the compromise between:
- Those who wanted strong protection for individual rights
- Those who wanted to limit federal jurisdiction
- Those who wanted to preserve state authority

---

SENATE DELIBERATIONS

SEPTEMBER 1789: SENATE CONSOLIDATION

The Senate took the 17 House amendments and further consolidated them into 12 proposed amendments. The Senate:

1. Combined some provisions that the House had kept separate
2. Refined language for legal precision
3. Addressed concerns about federal vs. state jurisdiction
4. Focused on amendments that commanded broad support

SEPTEMBER 9, 1789: SENATE APPROVAL

The Senate approved 12 amendments on September 9, 1789. These 12 amendments represented:
- A balance between protecting individual rights and respecting state authority
- A focus on the most essential protections
- Language that would be durable and clear

---

CONFERENCE COMMITTEE RESOLUTION

SEPTEMBER 1789: JOINT CONFERENCE COMMITTEE

A conference committee composed of three senators and three representatives worked out a compromise agreement consisting of twelve amendments. This committee:

1. Reconciled House and Senate versions
2. Resolved disagreements over specific language
3. Ensured broad consensus on final proposals

---

FINAL CONGRESSIONAL APPROVAL

Both the Senate and the House of Representatives passed the final twelve amendments and sent them to the President to be presented to the states for ratification.

---

AMENDMENTS THAT DID NOT SURVIVE

MADISON'S ORIGINAL PROPOSALS THAT WERE DROPPED OR MODIFIED

1. PREAMBLE ON SOURCE OF GOVERNMENT AUTHORITY
Madison wanted to preface the Constitution with a declaration that "all power is originally vested in, and consequently derived from the people." This preamble language was eliminated in favor of focusing on specific rights protections.

2. FEDERAL RESTRICTIONS ON STATE GOVERNMENTS
Madison proposed that "the equal rights of conscience, the freedom of the press, and the freedom of peaceful assembly and petition" should be protected against infringement by the states as well as the federal government.

The House approved this concept, but the Senate rejected it. The Bill of Rights, as ratified, applied only to federal government action, not to state governments. This limitation would not be changed until after the Civil War with the Fourteenth Amendment.

3. RELIGIOUS EXEMPTION FROM MILITARY SERVICE
The conscientious objector clause protecting those "religiously scrupulous of bearing arms" from compulsory military service was removed during the legislative process.

4. MONOPOLIES RESTRICTION
Jefferson and others had proposed restrictions on monopolies, but this amendment did not survive the congressional debates.

---

DEBATE THEMES AND ARGUMENTS

FEDERALIST ARGUMENTS AGAINST OR FOR LIMITING AMENDMENTS

1. The Constitution already protected rights through its structure
2. A bill of rights was unnecessary because the federal government had limited powers
3. Any enumeration of rights might imply that other rights were not protected
4. The states had bills of rights to protect citizens

ANTI-FEDERALIST ARGUMENTS FOR AMENDMENTS

1. The Constitution lacked explicit protection for fundamental rights
2. Citizens needed assurance that their liberties would be protected
3. An independent judiciary needed clear constitutional authority to protect rights
4. Popular confidence in the new government required explicit rights protections
5. The principles of the Declaration of Independence demanded such protections

---

MADISON'S RHETORICAL STRATEGY

Madison's approach to securing passage:

1. COMPROMISE AND CONSULTATION
Madison reviewed the various state ratifying conventions' proposed amendments and incorporated the most important and broadly supported ones.

2. FOCUS ON ESSENTIAL RIGHTS
Madison narrowed the scope to amendments that commanded broad support rather than pushing for all his ideas.

3. REASSURANCE ABOUT GOVERNMENT STRUCTURE
Madison repeatedly assured opponents that these amendments would not alter the fundamental structure or powers of government.

4. APPEALS TO CONSTITUENT DEMANDS
Madison emphasized that amendments were necessary to satisfy "a respectable number of our constituents."

5. RELENTLESS PERSISTENCE
Contemporary accounts note that Madison "hounded his colleagues relentlessly" to secure passage, demonstrating his commitment to the project.

---

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE

The Congressional debates on the Bill of Rights reveal:

1. INITIAL OPPOSITION: Even with the Constitution ratified, there was significant debate about whether rights needed explicit protection
2. JAMES MADISON'S LEADERSHIP: Madison's skill in negotiating between competing interests and his persistence secured amendments that might otherwise have failed
3. COMPROMISE AND REFINEMENT: The amendments were refined through multiple stages of debate, with the final product representing genuine consensus
4. FEDERALIST FLEXIBILITY: Even strong Federalists like Roger Sherman were willing to compromise to secure the government's stability
5. FOCUS ON INDIVIDUAL RIGHTS: The final amendments prioritize protection of individual liberty over government restructuring
