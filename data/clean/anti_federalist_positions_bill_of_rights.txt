ANTI-FEDERALIST POSITIONS AND DEMANDS FOR A BILL OF RIGHTS
1787-1789

---

THE ANTI-FEDERALIST MOVEMENT

The Anti-Federalists were a coalition of political leaders and ordinary citizens who opposed the ratification of the Constitution as drafted. A critical minority insisted upon the addition of a bill of rights that would protect the rights of individual citizens, and demanded a new look at some of the Constitution's specific provisions.

TIMELINE OF ANTI-FEDERALIST OPPOSITION

The Anti-Federalist movement emerged during the debates over ratification, from the Constitutional Convention signing in September 1787 through the ratification debates of 1787-1788, and influenced the amendments demanded in 1788-1789.

---

CORE ANTI-FEDERALIST FEARS AND ARGUMENTS

ABSENCE OF EXPLICIT RIGHTS PROTECTIONS

Primary Concern:
The Constitution, as drafted and proposed for ratification, contained no bill of rights. Anti-Federalists viewed this as a fundamental deficiency that could endanger liberty.

Anti-Federalist Position:
"A bill of rights is what the people are entitled to against every government on earth. No just government should refuse or rest on inferences."

The Anti-Federalists followed the tradition of state bills of rights (which existed in most states) and the English Bill of Rights of 1689, which had explicitly enumerated rights that governments must respect.

CENTRALIZATION OF POWER

Anti-Federalist Concern:
The new federal government granted too much power to a central authority, removing protection from local control.

Specific Fears:
1. Standing armies controlled by the federal government
2. Federal taxation without limits
3. Federal courts with broad jurisdiction
4. A powerful executive
5. Dissolution of state sovereignty

Required Protection:
Anti-Federalists demanded enumerated rights that would limit federal government power, particularly:
- Protection against standing armies
- Protections in criminal justice
- Protections of property rights
- Protections of state authority

LACK OF ADEQUATE CHECKS AND BALANCES

Anti-Federalist Argument:
There were not enough checks and balances in the Constitution to protect citizens from governmental abuse of power.

Missing Protections:
1. Explicit prohibition on suspension of habeas corpus (except in emergencies)
2. Explicit protection of jury trial rights
3. Explicit protections against unreasonable searches
4. Explicit prohibitions on cruel punishment
5. Protections for freedom of conscience and speech

---

MAJOR ANTI-FEDERALIST WRITINGS AND DEMANDS

THE BRUTUS ESSAYS

One of the most widely known Anti-Federalist writings came from a series of sixteen essays published in the New York Journal from October 1787 through April 1788 under the pseudonym "Brutus."

Brutus's Arguments:
1. The federal government was granted too much power
2. Federal courts would undermine state courts
3. Congress's taxation powers were unlimited
4. A standing army was dangerous to liberty
5. A bill of rights was essential

Impact:
The Brutus essays were widely read and influential in shaping Anti-Federalist thought. They provided intellectual ammunition for those demanding a bill of rights as a condition of ratification.

OTHER ANTI-FEDERALIST PSEUDONYMS AND WRITERS

"The Federal Farmer" - Letters emphasizing the danger of unchecked federal power and demanding explicit rights protections

"Cato" - Essays from New York opposing the Constitution's centralization of power

"Centinel" - Pennsylvania anti-Federalist essays demanding a bill of rights

State-Based Anti-Federalists:
- George Mason of Virginia
- Patrick Henry of Virginia
- Samuel Adams of Massachusetts
- Others who influenced state ratifying conventions

---

STATE RATIFICATION CONVENTION CONDITIONS

MASSACHUSETTS COMPROMISE (1788)

The Massachusetts ratifying convention, where Anti-Federalist sentiment was strong, produced a crucial turning point through the following events:

Key Players:
- John Adams (at the time considered more moderate)
- John Hancock (viewed as skeptical of the Constitution)
- Other Anti-Federalist delegates

The Compromise:
Two leading Federalists negotiated with Anti-Federalist leaders, resulting in an agreement that:

1. Massachusetts would ratify the Constitution
2. The state would propose specific amendments for Congress to consider
3. The nation would understand that amendments addressing Anti-Federalist concerns would be added after ratification

Impact on Bill of Rights:
This Massachusetts compromise established a crucial precedent. Other states with strong Anti-Federalist populations would follow this example, expanding the list of demanded amendments. The Massachusetts approach allowed ratification to succeed while preserving the Anti-Federalist demand for rights protections.

VIRGINIA RATIFICATION CONVENTION (JUNE 1788)

Anti-Federalist Leaders:
- Patrick Henry - leading opponent of ratification without amendments
- George Mason - author of Virginia's Declaration of Rights (1776)

Virginia's Position:
Virginia proposed a comprehensive set of amendments that became influential in shaping the Bill of Rights, including:
- Protections for religious freedom
- Protections for freedom of speech and press
- Jury trial guarantees
- Protection against standing armies
- Protection against unreasonable searches
- Due process protections

NEW YORK RATIFICATION CONVENTION (JULY 1788)

Anti-Federalist Dominance:
New York had a strong Anti-Federalist majority initially opposed to ratification.

Proposed Amendments:
New York's ratifying convention proposed numerous amendments focused on:
- Explicit rights protections
- Limitations on federal power
- Guarantees of jury trials
- Protections against standing armies
- Protections of religious freedom

NORTH CAROLINA RATIFICATION CONVENTION (AUGUST 1788)

North Carolina's Anti-Federalist Convention:
North Carolina's initial ratifying convention, dominated by Anti-Federalists, actually rejected the Constitution and did not ratify until 1789 after amendments were proposed.

SPECIFIC ANTI-FEDERALIST DEMANDS FOR AMENDMENTS

RIGHTS TO BE EXPLICITLY PROTECTED

1. FREEDOM OF CONSCIENCE AND RELIGIOUS LIBERTY
Anti-Federalist Demand: Explicit protection against federal establishment of religion and guarantee of free exercise

"No person shall be compelled to render military service in person if conscientiously opposed to bearing arms; nor shall any person be compelled to pay a tax to support the worship of any religion opposite to their conscientious belief."

2. FREEDOM OF SPEECH AND PRESS
Anti-Federalist Demand: Explicit protection against federal suppression of speech and press

"The freedom of the press shall not be violated or restrained by any laws of the United States."

3. PROTECTION OF JURY TRIALS
Anti-Federalist Demand: Guarantee of jury trials in both civil and criminal cases

"No person shall be deprived of the trial by jury, in any case, civil or criminal, where the value in controversy exceeds twenty dollars."

4. PROTECTION AGAINST STANDING ARMIES
Anti-Federalist Demand: Severe limitations on federal military forces

"A well regulated militia, composed of the body of the people, being the best security of a free state, shall not be disarmed."

Additionally: "No standing army shall be kept up in time of peace without the consent of Congress."

5. PROTECTIONS IN CRIMINAL PROCEDURES
Anti-Federalist Demands:
- Grand jury requirement for federal prosecutions
- Protection against self-incrimination
- Right to counsel
- Speedy and public trial
- Confrontation of witnesses
- Protection against double jeopardy

6. PROTECTIONS AGAINST UNREASONABLE SEARCHES
Anti-Federalist Demand: Explicit protection against federal searches without warrants

"The rights of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers and effects, against all unreasonable seizures of their person, or seizure of their papers or property, shall not be violated."

7. PROPERTY RIGHTS AND JUST COMPENSATION
Anti-Federalist Demand: Protection that private property could not be taken for public use without just compensation

8. LIMITATIONS ON FEDERAL POWER
Anti-Federalist Demand: A reservation that powers not granted to the federal government were reserved to the states or to the people

ANTI-FEDERALIST POSITIONS ON PROPOSED AMENDMENTS

WHICH AMENDMENTS WERE MOST IMPORTANT?

Based on state convention proposals, Anti-Federalists ranked the following as most essential:

1. Explicit religious freedom protection (demanded by most states)
2. Trial by jury guarantees (demanded by most states)
3. Protection against unreasonable searches (demanded by Virginia, North Carolina, New York)
4. Protection against standing armies (demanded by Virginia, New York, North Carolina)
5. Due process protections (demanded by multiple states)
6. Freedom of speech and press (demanded by multiple states)

WHICH AMENDMENTS THEY WOULD HAVE PREFERRED

Anti-Federalists proposed more amendments than were ultimately adopted. They wanted:

1. APPLICATION TO THE STATES
Anti-Federalists wanted amendments that would restrict state governments as well as the federal government. Madison had proposed this, but the Senate rejected it. This limitation was only corrected with the Fourteenth Amendment after the Civil War.

2. FEDERAL STRUCTURE CHANGES
Some Anti-Federalists wanted more fundamental changes to federal structure:
- Direct election of senators
- Limitation on re-election of the president
- Greater restrictions on federal taxation power
- Requirements for supermajorities on certain federal actions

3. MONOPOLIES RESTRICTION
Jefferson and others had proposed restrictions on monopolies, which some Anti-Federalists supported.

These more structural changes did not survive the amendment process, though later amendments would address some (such as direct election of senators in the Seventeenth Amendment).

---

ANTI-FEDERALIST INFLUENCE ON THE FINAL BILL OF RIGHTS

FUNDAMENTAL IMPACT

The Bill of Rights, as finally adopted, reflects the core Anti-Federalist demands for explicit protection of individual rights. The first amendments address the Anti-Federalist agenda:

- Amendment I: Freedom of conscience, speech, press, assembly (Anti-Federalist priority)
- Amendment II: Right to bear arms, militia context (Anti-Federalist priority)
- Amendment III: Protection against quartering (Anti-Federalist priority)
- Amendment IV: Protection against unreasonable searches (Anti-Federalist priority)
- Amendment V: Due process, protection against self-incrimination (Anti-Federalist priority)
- Amendment VI: Trial by jury, speedy trial, counsel (Anti-Federalist priority)
- Amendment VIII: Protection against cruel punishment (Anti-Federalist priority)
- Amendment IX: Retention of unenumerated rights (Anti-Federalist concern addressed)
- Amendment X: Reservation of powers (Anti-Federalist priority)

WHAT ANTI-FEDERALISTS ACHIEVED

The Anti-Federalist movement successfully:
1. Forced the new government to explicitly commit to individual rights protections
2. Created a constitutional document (with amendments) that provided greater protections for liberty than existed before
3. Established the precedent that government power should be limited by enumerated rights
4. Created the foundation for an independent judiciary to protect constitutional rights
5. Demonstrated that the ratified Constitution could be amended to address legitimate concerns

---

THE TRANSFORMATION OF THE ANTI-FEDERALIST VISION

While not all Anti-Federalist proposals became amendments, the Bill of Rights represented a significant victory for Anti-Federalist principles. The amendments:

1. Established that individual rights would be explicitly protected
2. Created constitutional limits on federal government power in crucial areas
3. Reserved significant power to the states and the people
4. Provided the framework for constitutional protection of liberty

As a result, many Anti-Federalists who had opposed the Constitution came to accept it once the Bill of Rights was adopted. The amendments transformed the Constitution from an instrument that Anti-Federalists feared into one that included their core concerns about protection of individual liberty.

---

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE

The Anti-Federalist movement and their demands for a Bill of Rights demonstrate:

1. The power of organized opposition to force necessary compromises
2. The importance of constitutional protections for individual rights
3. The flexibility of the new constitutional framework to accommodate legitimate concerns
4. The enduring tension between centralized power and individual liberty in American politics

The Anti-Federalists, though they lost the ratification battle, won the peace through their insistence on a bill of rights that would shape American constitutional law for centuries.
