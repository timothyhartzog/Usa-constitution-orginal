KEY FIGURES IN THE BILL OF RIGHTS
1787-1791

---

JAMES MADISON (1751-1836)
"Father of the Constitution" and Primary Author of the Bill of Rights

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
James Madison was a delegate to the Constitutional Convention, a member of the First Congress, and the primary architect of the Bill of Rights. He was born in Port Conway, Virginia, and played a central role in both the constitutional and post-constitutional history of the United States.

MADISON'S TRANSFORMATION ON RIGHTS PROTECTION

Initial Position (1787-1788):
Madison initially opposed a bill of rights, arguing that:
1. A bill of rights would be merely "a parchment barrier" providing no real protections
2. The enumeration of rights might be incomplete
3. The Constitution's structure and separation of powers were sufficient safeguards
4. Written protections could not guarantee liberty if the government was disposed to violate them

Reasons for Change:
1. Correspondence with Thomas Jefferson persuaded him of the philosophical importance of rights
2. Observations of the ratification debates showed widespread demand for amendments
3. Recognition that a bill of rights could serve educational purposes
4. Fear that without amendments, there would be pressure for a second constitutional convention that might drastically weaken the federal government
5. Understanding that a bill of rights could provide an independent judiciary with clear authority to protect rights

Madison's Resolution (1789):
Madison "hounded his colleagues relentlessly" to secure passage of amendments, demonstrating his total commitment to the project.

MADISON'S CONTRIBUTIONS TO THE BILL OF RIGHTS

1. COMPILATION OF PROPOSALS
Madison reviewed:
- State ratifying convention proposals
- Anti-Federalist demands
- Articles of Confederation protections
- State bills of rights
- Historical precedents

2. DRAFTING
Madison prepared nine proposed amendments (thirty-nine items when counted individually) that addressed the most pressing concerns without restructuring government powers.

3. CONGRESSIONAL LEADERSHIP
Madison:
- Introduced amendments on June 8, 1789
- Defended them against criticism
- Negotiated with committee members
- Made concessions where necessary to secure passage
- Persuaded reluctant colleagues of their importance

4. KEY LANGUAGE
Madison's language for critical amendments:
- Religious freedom: "the equal rights of conscience, the freedom of the press, and the freedom of peaceful assembly"
- Due process: protection of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law"
- Unenumerated rights: The Ninth Amendment protecting rights not enumerated
- State powers: The Tenth Amendment reserving powers to states or people

5. UNSUCCESSFUL PROPOSALS
Madison proposed but Congress rejected:
- A preamble on the source of government authority
- Application of rights protections to the states (not achieved until the Fourteenth Amendment)
- Conscientious objector protection for military service
- Restrictions on monopolies

MADISON'S LEGACY
Madison established that:
- Rights protections should be explicit and constitutional
- An independent judiciary should have authority to enforce them
- Amendments could be used to address legitimate constitutional concerns
- Compromise and negotiation could produce documents of lasting significance

THOMAS JEFFERSON (1743-1826)
Philosopher and Advocate of Rights Protection

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
Thomas Jefferson, author of the Declaration of Independence and Minister to France during the Constitution's drafting and the Bill of Rights' creation, was the intellectual force behind the demand for a bill of rights.

JEFFERSON'S ROLE

1. PHILOSOPHICAL ADVOCACY
Jefferson's correspondence with James Madison provided crucial philosophical support for the bill of rights, asserting:
- "A bill of rights is what the people are entitled to against every government on earth"
- "No just government should refuse, or rest on inferences"
- Rights protection is a matter of justice, not optional governance

2. SPECIFIC PROPOSALS
Jefferson advocated for explicit protection of:
- Freedom of religion
- Freedom of the press
- Protection against standing armies
- Restriction against monopolies
- The habeas corpus guarantee
- Trial by jury

3. PERSUASION OF MADISON
Through persistent correspondence (1787-1789), Jefferson gradually changed Madison's mind about the necessity of a bill of rights. This intellectual exchange between two of America's greatest thinkers influenced the course of constitutional history.

4. INFLUENCE FROM ABROAD
Though in Paris as Minister to France, Jefferson's ideas circulated among American political leaders and influenced state convention proposals for amendments.

JEFFERSON'S SPECIFIC CONTRIBUTIONS

Letters to James Madison (December 20, 1787, and other dates):
"The omission of a bill of rights providing clearly and without the aid of sophisms for freedom of religion, freedom of the press, protection against standing armies, restriction against monopolies, the eternal and unremitting force of the habeas corpus laws, and trials by jury in all matters of fact triable by the laws of the land and not by the law of Nations."

JEFFERSON'S LEGACY
Jefferson established the principle that:
- Individual rights are matters of justice, not political convenience
- Rights protections should be explicit and constitutional
- A bill of rights should be more than "a parchment barrier"—it should be law
- An educated citizenry understanding their rights is essential to liberty

GEORGE WASHINGTON (1732-1799)
President, Advocate for Amendments

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
George Washington, President of the Constitutional Convention and first President of the United States, played a crucial role in ensuring that amendments were considered.

WASHINGTON'S CONTRIBUTIONS

1. POLITICAL COMMITMENT
Washington personally pledged to Madison that amendments would be considered, recognizing that:
- Some amendments would be necessary to reduce Anti-Federalist opposition
- A second constitutional convention might drastically alter and weaken the new federal government
- Public confidence in the new government required addressing concerns about rights

2. CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY
Washington's presidential authority and prestige lent credibility to the amendment process:
- He presented the amendments to Congress
- He transmitted the amendments to the states for ratification
- His administration shepherded the amendments through ratification

3. ENCOURAGEMENT TO CONGRESS
Washington supported Madison's efforts to secure passage, understanding that amendments were necessary for the stability and longevity of the new government.

WASHINGTON'S LEGACY
Washington demonstrated that:
- Even those who opposed a bill of rights could recognize its political and constitutional value
- Constitutional amendments could be used to strengthen, not weaken, the fundamental document
- Leadership requires recognizing when opposition represents legitimate concerns

ROGER SHERMAN (1721-1793)
Federalist Opponent and Procedural Shaper

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
Roger Sherman of Connecticut was a signer of the Declaration of Independence, the Articles of Confederation, and the Constitution. He was a leading Federalist who opposed unnecessary amendments but ultimately accepted them.

SHERMAN'S POSITION

1. CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY ARGUMENT
Sherman argued that Congress might not have constitutional authority to propose changes to the Constitution's wording. This argument, though ultimately rejected, raised important constitutional questions about the amendment process.

2. STATE RIGHTS ARGUMENT
Sherman reassured the convention that the states had their own bills of rights and therefore had no need for a national bill of rights. This argument reflected Federalist confidence in state governments and in the Constitution's structure.

3. DOCUMENT INVIOLABILITY CONCERN
Sherman was concerned about violating the structural integrity of the Constitution document itself. He persuaded the House to place the amendments at the Constitution's end rather than interpolating them within the text.

This decision meant:
- The original Constitution remained unchanged
- Amendments appeared as a separate, identifiable section
- The Constitution would "remain inviolate" in its original form

SHERMAN'S LEGACY
Sherman demonstrated that:
- Even strong opponents of amendments could accept them for the sake of constitutional stability
- Procedural considerations matter in constitutional governance
- Federalist flexibility and pragmatism could accommodate legitimate concerns

PATRICK HENRY (1736-1799)
Anti-Federalist Leader and Rights Advocate

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
Patrick Henry was a leading Anti-Federalist who opposed the Constitution as drafted because of its lack of explicit rights protections. His famous declaration "Give me liberty, or give me death" captured his lifelong commitment to individual freedom.

HENRY'S ROLE

1. OPPOSITION TO THE CONSTITUTION
Henry was the principal opponent of the Constitution in Virginia's ratifying convention, arguing that the document contained insufficient protections for individual rights and granted too much power to a central government.

2. DEMAND FOR AMENDMENTS
Henry's powerful speeches and arguments in the Virginia convention helped persuade delegates that amendments would be necessary. His argument that the Constitution lacked a bill of rights was instrumental in Virginia proposing comprehensive amendments.

3. INFLUENCE ON STATE CONVENTIONS
Virginia, under Henry's influence, proposed detailed amendments focusing on:
- Religious freedom
- Freedom of speech and press
- Jury trial guarantees
- Protection against standing armies
- Protection against unreasonable searches
- Due process protections

These proposals significantly influenced the final Bill of Rights.

HENRY'S LEGACY
Henry demonstrated that:
- Opposition to government overreach could motivate constitutional improvements
- Individual rights were so important that they justified threatening to withhold ratification
- Principled leadership could force governmental accommodation to legitimate concerns

GEORGE MASON (1725-1792)
Anti-Federalist Delegate and Rights Pioneer

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
George Mason was the author of the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776), one of the most important state bills of rights that preceded the Bill of Rights. He was a delegate to the Constitutional Convention and a leading Anti-Federalist who refused to sign the Constitution.

MASON'S CONTRIBUTIONS

1. CONSTITUTIONAL CONVENTION
Mason was present at the Constitutional Convention but walked out in opposition to the Constitution because it lacked a bill of rights. He subsequently actively opposed ratification.

2. VIRGINIA DECLARATION OF RIGHTS (1776)
Mason's earlier Virginia Declaration of Rights served as a model for both state bills of rights and influenced the federal Bill of Rights. Key provisions from Mason's 1776 document appeared in the 1791 Bill of Rights:
- Religious freedom
- Freedom of press
- Trial by jury
- Protection against cruel punishment
- Due process protections

3. ANTI-FEDERALIST PERSUASION
Mason's status as a respected founder and his principled opposition to ratification without amendments lent credibility to Anti-Federalist demands.

4. INFLUENCE ON STATE PROPOSALS
Mason's ideas and language influenced the amendments proposed by state ratifying conventions, particularly Virginia's comprehensive list of amendments.

MASON'S LEGACY
Mason established that:
- A bill of rights should be fundamental to any free government
- Rights protections needed to be explicit and constitutional
- The model of a state bill of rights could serve as a foundation for national protections
- Principled opposition, even from founding figures, could improve constitutional governance

JOHN ADAMS (1735-1826)
Massachusetts Leader and Negotiator

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
John Adams was a leading figure in Massachusetts politics during the ratification period. Though not directly involved in drafting the Bill of Rights, Adams played a crucial role in Massachusetts's acceptance of the Constitution with amendments.

ADAMS'S ROLE

1. MASSACHUSETTS COMPROMISE
Adams negotiated with Anti-Federalist leaders John Hancock and others to produce the Massachusetts compromise:
- Massachusetts would ratify the Constitution
- The state would propose specific amendments for Congress to consider
- The nation would understand that amendments addressing Anti-Federalist concerns would be added

2. TRANSFORMATION OF RATIFICATION PROCESS
Adams's successful negotiation in Massachusetts established a precedent that influenced other state conventions. The Massachusetts approach allowed ratification while preserving the demand for amendments.

3. INFLUENCE ON THE AMENDMENT TIMELINE
Adams's compromise meant that:
- The Constitution could be ratified without all Anti-Federalist objections being resolved
- The First Congress would immediately address the amendment question
- State conventions' proposed amendments would guide the congressional process

ADAMS'S LEGACY
Adams demonstrated that:
- Political compromise and negotiation could address fundamental disagreements
- Constitutional amendments could be used as a tool to achieve broader consensus
- Leadership meant finding ways to satisfy legitimate concerns

JOHN HANCOCK (1737-1793)
Massachusetts Delegate and Bridge-Builder

BIOGRAPHICAL SUMMARY
John Hancock was an influential Massachusetts political leader whose position on the Constitution was initially unclear. His agreement to support ratification with proposed amendments was crucial to Massachusetts's vote.

HANCOCK'S ROLE

1. MASSACHUSETTS RATIFICATION INFLUENCE
Hancock's statement that he would support ratification with proposed amendments helped persuade the Massachusetts convention to vote for ratification despite strong Anti-Federalist sentiment.

2. PROPOSING AMENDMENTS
Hancock and other Massachusetts delegates agreed to propose amendments and then accepted the compromise proposed by Adams and other Federalists.

3. MODEL FOR OTHER STATES
Hancock's position established that state leaders could accept the Constitution while still advocating for amendments, a position that other states then adopted.

HANCOCK'S LEGACY
Hancock demonstrated that:
- Even influential Anti-Federalists could find grounds for accepting the Constitution
- Proposing amendments was a legitimate way to improve the Constitution
- State leadership could facilitate broader constitutional consensus

LESSER-KNOWN BUT IMPORTANT FIGURES

JOHN ADAMS (Massachusetts)
Led negotiations for the Massachusetts compromise that established the pattern for ratification with proposed amendments.

SPENCER ROANE (Virginia)
Virginia attorney and judge who influenced the state's proposed amendments.

RICHARD HENRY LEE (Virginia)
Anti-Federalist leader whose "Letters from the Federal Farmer" influenced demands for amendments.

CORNELIUS VAN ALLEN VAN SCOY (Pennsylvania)
Pennsylvania delegate who advocated for strong rights protections.

COMMITTEE MEMBERS (1789)
The eleven-member House committee that refined Madison's proposals included:
- Representatives who refined the language of amendments
- Those who balanced individual rights with federalism concerns
- Framers who helped shape the final language

INTERACTION OF KEY FIGURES

THE JEFFERSON-MADISON DYNAMIC

The most important intellectual partnership was between Jefferson and Madison:
- Jefferson provided philosophical foundation and specific proposals
- Madison provided political skill and negotiating ability
- Together, they created the intellectual and practical basis for the Bill of Rights

FEDERALIST-ANTI-FEDERALIST NEGOTIATION

The tension between Federalists (like Sherman and Adams) and Anti-Federalists (like Henry and Mason) produced:
- Legitimate demands for amendments
- Careful attention to the government's structural integrity
- A final product that both sides could accept

THE WASHINGTON BACKDROP

Washington's presidency and his personal pledges to Madison provided:
- Constitutional authority for the amendment process
- Confidence that amendments would not weaken the government
- Administrative machinery for ratification

---

HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE

The key figures in the Bill of Rights saga demonstrate:

1. HOW INDIVIDUAL LEADERSHIP MATTERS
Madison's persistence, Jefferson's intellectual influence, and Washington's authority made the Bill of Rights possible.

2. HOW COMPROMISE WORKS
The interaction of Federalists and Anti-Federalists, initial opponents and ultimate supporters, produced a document that became fundamental to American liberty.

3. HOW AMENDMENTS STRENGTHEN CONSTITUTIONS
Rather than weakening the Constitution, the Bill of Rights strengthened it by addressing legitimate concerns and broadening support for the new government.

4. HOW PRINCIPLES AND PRAGMATISM INTERACT
The figures involved balanced:
- Principled commitment to individual rights (Jefferson, Henry, Mason)
- Pragmatic concerns about government stability (Washington, Sherman, Adams)
- Negotiating skill to achieve compromise (Madison, Adams)
