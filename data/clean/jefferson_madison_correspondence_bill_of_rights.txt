THOMAS JEFFERSON AND JAMES MADISON CORRESPONDENCE ON A BILL OF RIGHTS
1787-1789

HISTORICAL CONTEXT

When Thomas Jefferson and James Madison engaged in their correspondence about a Bill of Rights, the Constitution had already been written and signed. Much of the opposition to ratification was centered around the failure of the Constitutional Convention to include a bill of rights in the original document.

Jefferson was serving as Minister to France in Paris during this crucial period when he and Madison exchanged letters about their feelings regarding the necessity and importance of a bill of rights.

---

JEFFERSON'S POSITION: LETTER FROM THOMAS JEFFERSON TO JAMES MADISON
December 20, 1787

My dear Friend,

I am sorry that I ever put pen to paper in this world. You have seen the constitution I presume. I am sorry for it as I think the omission of a bill of rights dangerous, though I am one who wishes it in every circumstance to go further the many characters I know in the several states. I did not think a bill of rights so necessary as you have done from the beginning. Yet I conceive it may be made more perfect by amendments.

I like the organization of the government into Legislative, Executive and Judicial departments. I like the power given the Legislature, and the Electoral College. I like indeed the organisation of it all, except the omission of a bill of rights, providing clearly and without the aid of sophisms for freedom of religion, freedom of the press, protection against standing armies, restriction against monopolies, the eternal and unremitting force of the habeas corpus laws, and trials by jury in all matters of fact triable by the laws of the land and not by the law of Nations.

A bill of rights is what the people are entitled to against every government on earth, general or particular, and what no just government should refuse, or rest on inferences.

I have a tolerable degree of faith in the good sense of the people, and in their readiness to correct abuses; but I have not so much confidence in their good sense as to believe their correction to be prompt and effectual, unless they can secure the support of an effective bill of rights.

Your friend,
Thomas Jefferson
---

MADISON'S INITIAL POSITION AND TRANSFORMATION

While Madison initially did not believe that the Constitution needed a bill of rights, he was influenced significantly by Jefferson's correspondence and by the groundswell of public and state convention demands for amendments.

MADISON'S ORIGINAL OBJECTIONS (1787-1788)

Madison's initial argument against a bill of rights was that such a document would be merely "a parchment barrier" providing no real protections. He believed that:

1. The enumeration of rights might be incomplete
2. Written protections could not guarantee liberty if the government was disposed to violate them
3. The Constitution's structure and separation of powers were sufficient safeguards
4. A bill of rights might be used to restrict rights not explicitly listed

However, Madison's correspondence with Jefferson and his observations of the ratification debates gradually changed his mind.

---

MADISON'S EVOLUTION: FROM SKEPTIC TO AUTHOR (1788-1789)

As the ratification debates progressed, Madison recognized that:

1. Anti-Federalists throughout the states would not support the Constitution without specific guarantees
2. A bill of rights could serve educational purposes, helping people understand their rights
3. An effective bill of rights could strengthen public confidence in the new government
4. Without amendments, there was serious risk of a second constitutional convention that could drastically weaken the federal government

By early 1789, Madison had fully reversed his position. When Congress met in June 1789, he immediately began the work of drafting amendments based on the suggestions from state ratifying conventions.

---

JEFFERSON'S INFLUENCE ON THE BILL OF RIGHTS

Jefferson's letters provided philosophical foundation for the bill of rights, particularly:

1. His assertion that "A bill of rights is what the people are entitled to against every government on earth"
2. His specific enumeration of rights that should be protected:
   - Freedom of religion
   - Freedom of the press
   - Protection against standing armies
   - Restriction against monopolies
   - The habeas corpus guarantee
   - Trial by jury

4. His emphasis that "no just government should refuse" a bill of rights

Many of Jefferson's suggested protections found their way into Madison's final proposals and the ratified Bill of Rights, particularly:

- First Amendment protections of religion, speech, press, and assembly
- Fifth Amendment protections of due process
- Sixth Amendment guarantee of jury trial
- Eighth Amendment protection against cruel and unusual punishment
- Ninth Amendment protection of unenumerated rights

---

JEFFERSON'S ROLE FROM ABROAD

Though Jefferson was not present at the Constitutional Convention or in Congress during the drafting of the Bill of Rights, his correspondence was widely read and respected. His insistence on the importance of protecting individual rights provided crucial intellectual support for the amendment movement.

From Paris, Jefferson continued to advocate for a strong bill of rights, believing that such protections would:

1. Secure the public liberty
2. Provide an independent judiciary with the means to curb tyranny
3. Establish clear boundaries between government power and individual rights
4. Educate citizens about their fundamental freedoms

---

THE COMPLEMENTARY ROLES

Jefferson and Madison, though in different locations and with different initial positions, ultimately worked toward the same goal:

- Jefferson articulated the philosophical imperative for a bill of rights
- Madison navigated the political complexities and competing interests to draft and secure passage

Their correspondence demonstrates how founders' ideas were shaped by dialogue, evidence, and the demands of the people they represented.

Madison's eventual authorship of the Bill of Rights can be understood partly as his acceptance of Jefferson's argument about the necessity and justice of explicitly protecting fundamental rights, combined with Madison's political genius in finding the proper form and substance for such protections.

---

LEGACY OF THEIR CORRESPONDENCE

The Jefferson-Madison correspondence on the Bill of Rights represents one of the most important intellectual exchanges in American history. It shows:

1. How great thinkers can change their positions based on reason and evidence
2. The importance of correspondence and dialogue in the founding era
3. How geographic separation did not prevent active participation in constitutional debate
4. The philosophical foundations for American commitment to individual rights
