OBJECTIONS TO THE CONSTITUTION By George Mason I have objected to this Constitution as it now stands, principally on these grounds: First, That there is no declaration of rights. Secondly, That the power of Congress to call forth the militia to execute the laws of the union, suppress insurrections, and redress grievances, is unlimited and dangerous. Thirdly, That under the power to regulate commerce, Congress may impose taxes on exports.

The first thing I have to observe is, that the federal government ought to have as little power as possible over the general concerns of the union, and the several states ought to retain as much power as possible. The new constitution appears to me to be founded on different principles, and to sacrifice the rights of the states to the general government.

BRUTUS - THE FIRST To the Citizens of the State of New-York When the public is called to investigate and decide upon a question in which not only the present members of the community are deeply interested, but upon which the happiness and misery of generations yet unborn is in great measure dependent, the benevolence of that being whose sanction is essential to the formation of any government, as well as its prosperity, is necessarily supposed to be interested in its determination.FEDERALIST NO. 10 - JAMES MADISON Among the numerous advantages promised by a well-constructed union, none deserves to be more accurately developed than its tendency to break and control the violence of faction. The friend of popular governments never finds himself so much alarmed for their character and fate as when he contemplates their propensity to this dangerous vice. He will not fail, therefore, to set a due value on any plan which, without violating the principles to which he is attached, provides a proper cure for it.

By faction I understand a number of citizens, whether amounting to a minority or majority of the whole, who are united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights of other citizens. There are two methods of curing the mischiefs of faction: the one, by removing its causes; the other, by controlling its effects.

FEDERALIST NO. 51 - JAMES MADISON To what expedient, then, shall we finally resort, for maintaining in practice the necessary partition of power among the several departments as laid down in the constitution? The answer that suggests itself is that as the exterior provision is first to be made by so contriving the interior structure of the government as that its several constituent parts may, by their mutual relations, be the means of keeping each other in their proper places.OBJECTIONS TO THE CONSTITUTION By George Mason I have objected to this Constitution as it now stands, principally on these grounds: First, That there is no declaration of rights.
