Political attachments, alliances, and antagonisms among the fifty-five delegates to the Federal Convention of 1787 — rendered, dissected, and annotated.

I. The Five FactionsAnatomy of the room

❦

Most popular accounts of the Constitutional Convention reduce it to a contest between Federalists and Anti-Federalists. This is a retrojection from the ratification debates of 1787–88. Inside Independence Hall that summer, the cleavages ran on at least four axes simultaneously — nationalism vs. confederalism, large states vs. small, slaveholding South vs. commercial North, and (cutting across all of these) personal temperament, regional pride, and old Revolutionary loyalties. What follows is a working taxonomy of how the men actually clustered.

The Nationalists

Core · 22 men

Madison, Washington, Hamilton, Wilson, Gouverneur Morris, Robert Morris, King, Gorham, Pinckneys (both), Rutledge, Butler, Read, Mifflin, Clymer, Fitzsimons, Ingersoll, Franklin (moderating), Blair, Williamson, McHenry, Carroll. They wanted a genuinely sovereign national government with direct authority over individuals, not a league of states. They drove the Virginia Plan and the final architecture.

The Connecticut Brokers

Pivot · 3 men

Sherman, Ellsworth, Johnson. The single most consequential bloc in the room. They were nationalist enough to want a real government and small-state enough to demand equal representation in one chamber. They proposed the compromise of July 16 that broke the deadlock and made the Constitution possible. Without them there is no Senate, no Constitution.

The Small-State Resistance

Defensive bloc · 11 men

Paterson, Brearley, Dayton, Livingston (NJ); Bedford, Bassett, Broom, Dickinson, Read (DE, though Read drifted nationalist); Luther Martin, Mercer (MD). They produced the New Jersey Plan on June 15 as an explicit counter to Madison's Virginia Plan. Bedford on June 30 threatened that small states would "find some foreign ally" rather than be crushed. They won the Senate and went home satisfied — mostly.

The Walkouts & Refusers

Dissent · 7 men

Yates and Lansing (NY) walked out July 10 and went home to warn Governor George Clinton that the convention had exceeded its mandate. Luther Martin and Mercer (MD) left in similar disgust. At the end, Gerry (MA), Mason (VA), and Randolph (VA) remained but refused to sign. Mason had been Madison's ally for months and broke at the last moment over the absence of a bill of rights.

The Deep South Bloc

Slavery axis · 8 men

Rutledge, both Pinckneys, and Butler (SC); Few, Baldwin, Houstoun, Pierce (GA). On most structural questions they sided with the Nationalists — but on slavery they were a phalanx. Rutledge in his August 21 speech made it brutally explicit: "Interest alone is the governing principle with nations. The true question at present is whether the Southern states shall or shall not be parties to the Union." They extracted the three-fifths clause, the 20-year slave-trade window, and the fugitive-slave clause as the price of their signatures.

The Absent in Body or Spirit

Drift · 9 men

Wythe and McClurg (VA), Houston (NJ), Strong (MA), Davie and Alexander Martin (NC), Houstoun and Pierce (GA, also above), Mercer (MD, also above). Family illness, business, and political discomfort thinned the room. Their absences mattered: had Strong stayed in Massachusetts's seat, Gerry's lone vote would have been less decisive on several key motions.

II. The Major AlliancesWho moved together

❦

The Virginia Triumvirate Madison · Washington · Randolph

The convention's center of gravity. Madison arrived eleven days early to coordinate strategy with the other Virginians at Mrs. House's boarding house on Fifth and Market. Washington — having agreed only reluctantly to attend — lent the Virginia Plan its legitimacy by sitting in the president's chair while Randolph stood and read it aloud on May 29. Madison wrote it; Randolph delivered it; Washington's silent presence sanctified it. By September the three were no longer one bloc: Randolph refused to sign, Washington signed first, and Madison had to manage the breach during ratification — which he eventually did when Randolph reversed himself at the Virginia ratifying convention in June 1788.

The Pennsylvania Intellectual Core Wilson · G. Morris · Franklin

James Wilson spoke 168 times, more than anyone except Gouverneur Morris (173). They were the convention's most relentless nationalists. Wilson grounded sovereignty in the people rather than the states — a radical move that made direct election of the lower house possible. Gouverneur Morris served on the Committee of Style and wrote the final language, including the magnificent preamble that opens "We the People." Franklin, at eighty-one, was carried into the hall in a sedan chair each day; he spoke rarely but every word counted. He brokered the Connecticut Compromise's framing, proposed daily prayers (defeated), and delivered the closing "rising sun" speech on September 17 that nudged the wavering toward signing.

The Connecticut Compromisers Sherman · Ellsworth · Johnson

Roger Sherman is the most underrated figure of the entire founding. A self-taught shoemaker's son, he is the only person to have signed the Continental Association, the Declaration of Independence, the Articles of Confederation, and the Constitution. He hated Madison's nationalism but accepted that the Confederation had failed. On June 11 he proposed the dual-representation scheme that would become the Great Compromise. Ellsworth refined it; Johnson — the urbane Anglican president of Columbia — sold it. Their bloc voted with the small states on representation and with the nationalists on most everything else, which is why they got their compromise: they were the median voter.

The Carolina-Georgia Slavery Phalanx Rutledge · Pinckneys · Butler · GA delegation

John Rutledge chaired the Committee of Detail (July 26 – August 6), which produced the first complete draft. He used the chair to insert explicit protections for the slave trade and against export taxes on the products of slave labor. Charles Cotesworth Pinckney — Rutledge's brother-in-law — handled the floor work. Pierce Butler, an Irish-born planter who owned hundreds of enslaved people, authored the fugitive-slave clause almost single-handedly on August 28. The Georgia delegation (Few, Baldwin, Houstoun, Pierce) supported them at every roll call. Without this alliance the Constitution does not exist; without it, also, much that came after does not exist either.

The Delaware Wall Read · Bedford · Bassett · Broom · Dickinson

Delaware's delegation operated under an explicit instruction from its state legislature: do not agree to any change in the one-state-one-vote rule. George Read led the bloc but quietly favored the nationalist program on every other issue. Gunning Bedford was the bloc's enforcer — short, fat, and famously hot-tempered. On June 30 he delivered the most threatening speech of the convention, warning the large states that the small ones would "find some foreign ally of more honor and good faith." John Dickinson, the famous "Penman of the Revolution," tried to keep the temperature down and counseled accommodation. The Delaware Wall held on representation; everywhere else it bent.

The South Carolina – Massachusetts Commercial Bargain Rutledge · King · Gorham

One of the convention's hidden architectures. On August 25, a secret deal was struck on the Committee of Eleven: the New England states agreed to drop their demand that navigation acts require two-thirds majorities (which would have crippled Southern shipping interests) in exchange for South Carolina dropping its demand for a permanent slave-trade guarantee. The compromise was a twenty-year window on the slave trade (until 1808) plus a simple majority for navigation acts. King and Gorham (MA) negotiated for the North; Rutledge and the Pinckneys for the South. Madison thought it was shameful. It passed.

III. The AntagonismsWho broke against whom

❦

Hamilton vs. His Own Delegation Hamilton · Yates · Lansing

The most painful arrangement in the room. New York had three delegates and voted as a bloc — and Hamilton was outvoted two-to-one on every state-level question by Robert Yates and John Lansing Jr., both creatures of Governor George Clinton, both committed states-rightsmen. On June 18, Hamilton delivered his famous six-hour speech proposing a president for life, senators for life, and state governors appointed by the federal government. Yates took the notes; they would surface during ratification to embarrass Hamilton. On July 10, Yates and Lansing simply left, depriving New York of its vote. Hamilton stayed on as an individual but New York had effectively withdrawn. He signed alone for his state on September 17.

Madison vs. Mason Allies who broke

For most of the summer, George Mason of Gunston Hall was Madison's senior partner in the Virginia delegation. He was sixty-two, the author of the Virginia Declaration of Rights of 1776 (which Madison had used as a template), and one of the convention's most prepared minds. They voted together on nearly everything until early September. Then, on September 12, Mason proposed that the Constitution be prefaced with a bill of rights. Gerry seconded him. The vote was ten states to zero against. Mason left the convention "in an exceeding ill humor" and refused to sign. He went home to Virginia and worked against ratification — to Madison's lasting bitterness, and to the eventual benefit of the country: the Bill of Rights of 1791 is partly Mason's vindication.

Madison vs. Sherman The structural antagonism

Not personal but constant. Sherman wanted a tweaked Confederation; Madison wanted a new sovereign. Sherman thought direct election of the lower house was dangerous ("the people should have as little to do as may be about the government"); Madison thought it indispensable. Sherman wanted state legislatures to choose senators (he got this); Madison wanted proportional representation in both houses (he lost this). When Madison wrote his retrospective notes, Sherman appears more often as an antagonist than anyone except perhaps Luther Martin.

Luther Martin vs. Almost Everyone The convention's gadfly

Maryland's attorney general spoke at extraordinary length, often drunkenly, and almost always in opposition. He delivered a two-day speech on June 27–28 against proportional representation that William Pierce of Georgia described as "delivered with much diffuseness, and in many places with great vehemence." Martin clashed with Madison, Wilson, Hamilton, and Randolph repeatedly. He left in early September and returned home to Maryland, where he wrote the "Genuine Information" — a long, furious account that became the most widely read Anti-Federalist text in the country. He hated the slave clauses, hated the Senate's small-state concession (he thought it wasn't enough), and hated the supremacy clause above all.

Gerry vs. The Nationalists A democrat with cold feet

Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts had been at the convention from the start and had voted with the nationalists on many early questions — but he grew steadily more alarmed as the summer wore on. He had been burned in Shays's Rebellion the previous winter, when his own constituents nearly came for him; he had begun to fear popular government as much as he feared aristocracy. On the final day he listed his objections: the powers of Congress were too vague, the executive's reeligibility too dangerous, the absence of a bill of rights intolerable. His name is the reason "gerrymander" exists (he signed an oddly shaped district map as Massachusetts governor in 1812). He, Mason, and Randolph are the three on the floor at the end who would not sign.

The Pinckney Cousin Rivalry C. Pinckney · C. C. Pinckney

Charles Pinckney was twenty-nine; Charles Cotesworth Pinckney was forty-one. They were second cousins, both from Charleston, both delegates from South Carolina. The younger Charles — vain, brilliant, and chronically self-promoting — submitted his own draft constitution on May 29 (the "Pinckney Plan") that he later claimed had been the basis for the final document. The claim is mostly false, but it bedeviled historians for a century. C. C. Pinckney was more measured, more respected, and quietly furious at his cousin's pretensions. They cooperated on the floor — they had to — but the family resentment ran for decades.

The Slavery Antagonism Mason · Madison vs. Rutledge · Pinckneys

The bitterest substantive fight of the convention, and the one most thoroughly papered over in the final text. Mason — himself the owner of roughly 300 enslaved people — denounced the slave trade in apocalyptic terms on August 22: "Every master of slaves is born a petty tyrant. They bring the judgment of heaven on a country." Madison shared the moral horror but was unwilling to break the convention over it. Rutledge replied that "religion and humanity have nothing to do with this question," and that South Carolina and Georgia would simply leave the Union if pressed. The Carolinas and Georgia won the immediate fight; the compromise (1808 importation cutoff) was struck four days later. The antagonism remained — and ripened, eventually, into civil war.

IV. The Hidden ArchitecturesCross-cutting alignments

❦

Age and Generation

The convention was younger than its statuary suggests. Franklin at eighty-one was the only octogenarian; Washington was fifty-five; Madison was thirty-six. The median delegate was forty-three. Jonathan Dayton of New Jersey, at twenty-six, was the youngest signer. The age structure mattered: the under-forties (Madison, Hamilton, Charles Pinckney, Gouverneur Morris, Dayton, Rutledge's protégés) were uniformly more nationalist than their elders, with the exception of Wilson, who at forty-five sat with them in spirit.

Wealth and the Economic Cleavage

Charles Beard's 1913 thesis that the Constitution was an economic document by and for creditors holds up only loosely. Yes, Robert Morris, Gouverneur Morris, and others held substantial public securities that would appreciate under a strong federal government. But the small-state delegates were not uniformly debtor-class, and Mason and Gerry — both wealthy — refused to sign. What is true: nearly every delegate owned land or enslaved people or both, and none of them represented the interests of small farmers, mechanics, or the unpropertied. The Constitution emerged from a roomful of property holders. Its biases follow.

Profession

Of fifty-five delegates, roughly thirty-four were trained in law; thirteen were merchants or financiers; eight were planters; four were physicians; three were soldiers by primary trade. The lawyers' dominance shows in the document's structure: it is a fundamentally legal text, organized around enumerated powers, jurisdictional grants, and procedural specifications, rather than a philosophical declaration in the manner of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man two years later. Hamilton said the Convention sometimes felt like "a court of chancery."

Religion and Reformed Habits of Mind

Most delegates were nominal Anglicans (after the Revolution, Episcopalians), with significant Presbyterian and Congregationalist contingents. Witherspoon-trained Princeton graduates (Madison, Paterson, Brearley, Davie, Ellsworth, Bedford, Houston, William Davie) made up a disproportionate share of the delegation: ten men. They brought a particular cast of mind — covenantal, suspicious of unchecked power, attentive to the depravity of human nature — that left fingerprints on the design of separation of powers and on Madison's Federalist 51. The Roman Catholic delegates were two (Daniel Carroll and Thomas Fitzsimons); the Jewish delegates zero; the explicitly Deist delegates probably two (Franklin and Wilson). On no occasion did the convention pray together: Franklin's June 28 motion for daily prayers was, in his own bitter parenthesis, "received but with one or two exceptions and the convention adjourned without coming to a determination."

V. The Final ReckoningWho signed, who did not, and why

❦

Of the fifty-five who attended, only forty-two were present on September 17. Three of those forty-two refused to sign: Mason, Gerry, Randolph. The other thirteen had already gone home — some over principle (Yates, Lansing, Luther Martin, Mercer), some over health or business (Wythe, McClurg, Houston, Strong, Houstoun, Davie, Alexander Martin, Pierce, Mifflin's brief absence aside).

The geography of signing tells a story. Every Pennsylvania delegate present signed; every Delaware delegate signed; every New Jersey delegate present signed; every Connecticut delegate present signed. New York signed only Hamilton (the others were gone). Massachusetts signed Gorham and King but lost Gerry. Virginia signed Washington, Madison, Blair — but lost Mason and Randolph (and Wythe and McClurg had left earlier). Maryland signed McHenry, Jenifer, Daniel Carroll — but lost Luther Martin and Mercer. The Carolinas and Georgia signed entire delegations.

"I confess that there are several parts of this constitution which I do not at present approve, but I am not sure I shall never approve them: For having lived long, I have experienced many instances of being obliged by better information, or fuller consideration, to change opinions even on important subjects, which I once thought right, but found to be otherwise." Benjamin Franklin, closing speech, September 17, 1787

Franklin's speech — read for him by Wilson, because Franklin no longer had the strength to deliver it himself — was an instrument of persuasion aimed at the three holdouts. It did not move them. But it moved the wavering in the middle, and after the signing was complete, Madison reports that Franklin looked at the sun carved into the back of Washington's chair and said he had often wondered, during the long summer, "whether it was rising or setting; but now at length I have the happiness to know that it is a rising and not a setting Sun." The remark closes Madison's notes. It is the convention's last recorded word.
